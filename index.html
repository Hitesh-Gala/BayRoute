A sovereign defence roadmap for India.

The rationale

Why India needs a sovereign defence roadmap

India remains heavily dependent on overseas suppliers for critical defence needs, even though it has the engineering base to design, develop and produce many advanced systems and platforms itself.

The cost of dependence

  • The Armed Forces rely on overseas suppliers more than they need to.
  • India's defence industry stays smaller than it could be.
  • The country lacks the leverage and independence that owning defence technology provides.

What sovereign capability provides

  • Simpler, more effective integration and networking of platforms and systems.
  • The ability to surge production during a conflict.
  • Stocks that can be replenished for India's own forces, or for a partner government.

The project

What must India be able to build by 2031?

Project BayRoute is a year-long study to identify the defence capabilities India should be able to design, develop, own and manufacture by 2031.

It works from first principles. Each stage builds on the last, so that the capabilities India pursues follow from the wars it may have to fight, not from the systems that happen to be on offer.

  1. 1

    The character of war

    How warfare is changing worldwide, and what that means for India.

  2. 2

    Conflicts and outcomes

    The conflicts India may face, and the outcomes it must be able to achieve.

  3. 3

    Strategies

    The military and strategic approaches that would achieve those outcomes.

  4. 4

    Capabilities

    What the Armed Forces need, set against India's strengths and critical dependencies.

  5. 5

    Sovereign industry

    Where domestic industry can build competitive, sovereign capability at scale.

Two fronts, considered together

The analysis considers plausible future conflicts involving Pakistan and China, including concurrent or connected crises. It also examines the technological, industrial and policy conditions needed to develop, produce and sustain the capabilities India needs, at scale.

A parallel to Project Meridian

In September 2026 the United States commissioned Project Meridian, a 120-day study of the technologies and capabilities its forces will need over the next two decades. BayRoute asks a similar question for India, and follows it through to the industry and policy needed to answer it.

On the name

BayRoute borrows its name, with affection, from a restaurant in Powai, Mumbai. Serious strategic conversations have often begun around a table.

Key questions

Five questions at the heart of the study

Together they run from how wars will be fought to what India must change to be ready for them.

  1. The future of conflict

    How is warfare likely to evolve over the next five to ten years, globally and in the Indian context? What do these changes mean for how India prepares for and conducts military operations?

  2. Strategies and outcomes

    What military and strategic outcomes must India be ready to achieve in plausible conflicts involving Pakistan and China, including concurrent or connected crises? What strategies would achieve them?

  3. Capability requirements

    What capabilities must the Armed Forces have to carry out these strategies and sustain operations under different conflict conditions?

  4. Sovereignty and competitiveness

    Which capabilities must India be able to design, control, integrate, modify and produce on its own? Where can it build technological and industrial advantages, and which critical dependencies should it reduce first?

  5. Policy and implementation

    What changes in policy, procurement, production and the wider defence ecosystem are needed to develop these capabilities by 2031?

How the study works

Research, tested against experience

  1. Analyse

    First-principles research into the character of war, India's likely conflicts and the capabilities they demand.

  2. Consult

    Discussions with serving and retired military officers, former government officials, manufacturers, technology developers and researchers. They help assess how warfare is changing, identify operational needs and critical dependencies, understand the constraints Indian industry faces, and test the study's emerging findings.

  3. Publish

    Findings are published at each stage and submitted to stakeholders in the Armed Forces and to the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Defence, alongside public writing and commentary.
